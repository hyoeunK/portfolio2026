Meta Horizon Worlds Preteen Account & Parental Supervision UX Design

Designed a safer social VR experience for preteens, paired with parental supervision tools that helped guardians stay informed while supporting an age-appropriate experience.

Context

Meta Quest needed age-appropriate tools, content, and protections for younger users entering immersive social spaces, including play, exploration, creation, events, and friend-based social experiences.

Problem

For preteens, immersive social features carried heightened risk: unfamiliar people, voice chat, virtual proximity, embodied interaction, and potential exposure to inappropriate content.

Challenge

The core question was how to design safety UX that balanced preteen agency, parent trust, and platform enjoyment without turning supervision into simple monitoring or blunt restriction.

Approach

I reframed safety from access restriction into a trust system: clear defaults, parent-managed decisions, transparent signals, and guardrails that preserved the core fun of VR.

Outcome

The work helped define an age-appropriate experience model where preteens could participate with stronger protections, while parents had the visibility and controls needed to guide their child's use.

My Scope

I worked with Legal, Policy, Family Center, and a three-person design team. My scope focused on the core user journey and UXR alignment, including request/approve flows, content ratings, notifications, and GenAI parental supervision features.

Design Strategy

01

Design age-appropriate defaults instead of simply adding restrictions. Adults, teens, and preteens needed different onboarding, permissions, and protection models that built trust from the start.

02

Turn access blocks into role transitions. The approval flow connected the preteen's VR screen, parent notification, review details, and Family Center into one cross-surface request loop.

03

Redesign safety for spatial and voice-based interaction. VR required guardrails for garbled voice, personal boundaries, hidden status/location, and known-people-first discovery.

04

Combine content ratings, child requests, and parent judgment. For UGC, ratings provided decision context so parents could make informed choices instead of relying on one-size-fits-all restrictions.

05

Position Family Center as a conversation starter, not a control panel. The experience focused on decision-ready signals, usage context, approvals, and simple education instead of more surveillance.

Twinning: Shared Identity as a Social Growth Loop

Designed a playful Easter-egg-like social feature for teen players who loved changing identity and customizing their look, turning shared self-expression into an in-world moment of recognition and connection.

Launch review footage shown for context; selected design explorations are omitted.

Context

Teen players could express identity through nameplate stickers, but those self-expression moments were mostly individual. The opportunity was to make shared identity visible when two people met in the same world.

Insight

Matching identity cues can create a tiny social spark: an excuse to notice someone, react, and start a lightweight interaction without needing a full conversation first.

Design Approach

I framed Twinning as a scalable recognition system, starting with matching nameplate stickers and leaving room for a later milestone around matching outfits. The work became prototype-heavy because the interaction depended on timing, distance, visibility, and how the effect appeared around moving players in VR.

Impact

After launch, friend adds between users who encountered each other through Twinning increased by nearly 20%. Because following relationships were strongly linked to retention, the feature became a meaningful social growth lever.

Prototyping & Spatial Design

This was a prototype-heavy project where the design lived in timing, distance, peripheral visibility, and how the effect behaved around moving players.

01

Tuned timing, angle, and visibility in Unity

I built Unity prototypes to test when the effect should trigger, how long it should last, and which viewing angles made it catch peripheral attention in VR.

02

Made delight noticeable, not disruptive

The effect needed to look fun to nearby players while staying lightweight enough not to interrupt movement, conversation, or ongoing play.

03

Explored group twinning moments

I tested whether larger effects could appear when multiple players matched, turning a one-to-one recognition moment into a shared group celebration.

04

Refined icons for spatial readability

I explored overhead icon placement, visual contrast, and background separation so the signal stayed readable across busy worlds and avatar positions.

What This Shows

Turned self-expression into social signals

The feature reframed identity items as triggers for recognition, reaction, and connection between players.

Designed a lightweight growth loop

Twinning created a playful in-world moment that led to more friend adds, strengthening a relationship graph tied to retention.

Scoped launch without losing the system

The team launched the nameplate version first while keeping the interaction model extensible for future outfit-based matching.

Balanced delight with product impact

The experience stayed small and playful on the surface, but connected directly to a measurable behavior that mattered for long-term engagement.

UGC Video Player for World Creation

A decision-focused design project about turning an ambiguous creator-tool problem into scoped product choices. I separated creator freedom from visitor experience, then framed the MVP as a learning system rather than a launch shortcut.

AI-generated concept image. Not Meta product imagery.

Context

World creators needed a way to place UGC video inside their worlds. The goal was not just to add a TV-like screen, but to make video a flexible screen object creators could scale, reshape, and use creatively as a phone, tablet, billboard, or spatial prop.

Challenge

Video in VR is shaped by screen scale, aspect ratio, control model, permissions, and visitor comfort. Small interaction choices could change both creator workflow and visitor experience.

Approach

I structured the ambiguity into options, then evaluated each one against two lenses: how much freedom it gave creators, and how predictable the experience stayed for visitors.

Recommendation

Ship a stable baseline—fixed aspect ratio and persistent controls—not as the safest shortcut, but as a learning system for observing real creator behavior before expanding flexibility.

Design Judgment

Structured ambiguity into options

I broke an open-ended creator-tool problem into concrete decisions around screen scale, control model, permissions, and launch sequencing.

Separated creator freedom from visitor quality

I evaluated flexibility and predictability as different constraints, so the MVP could protect viewing quality without closing off future creative use.

Designed MVP as a learning system

The recommendation created a stable launch baseline that could reveal how creators actually placed and used video in worlds.

Balanced control with predictability

For VR creator tools, I framed control as a spectrum: more creative power needs stronger discoverability, ownership, and permission logic.

Tradeoff Analysis 01 / Video Screen Scale

Recommended for MVP

Fixed Aspect Ratio

Most stable and predictable for visitors, with a consistent look across worlds.

Tradeoff: less flexible for creators and narrower support for varied video formats.

Creator Flexibility

Freeform

Lets creators treat video as a world-building object, scaling it into billboards, phones, stage screens, or ambient displays.

Tradeoff: shifts quality control to creators, increasing testing around legibility, distortion, viewing distance, and inconsistent playback surfaces.

Future Exploration

Auto Adjustment

Adapts the video surface to source format and viewing context, supporting landscape clips, vertical video, and spatial placements more gracefully.

Tradeoff: requires stronger rules for resizing, cropping, controls, and world-layout collisions, making it harder to validate as an MVP default.

Decision

Use fixed aspect ratio as the launch baseline to protect visitor viewing quality, then learn where creators actually need flexibility before expanding screen formats.

Tradeoff Analysis 02 / Control Model

Recommended for MVP

Persistent Panel

Anchors core playback controls and state near the video object, making control availability consistent across worlds.

Tradeoff: less immersive and less expressive for creators, but lowers learnability and support risk for first release.

Interaction Model

Raycast

Lets visitors point directly at the video surface, reducing persistent UI and feeling closer to native VR interaction.

Tradeoff: discoverability depends on distance, angle, scene clutter, and user intent, which can make playback feel unreliable.

Creator Control

Remote Panel

Gives creators a separate control surface for setup, moderation, or live-event playback without crowding the video object.

Tradeoff: introduces harder questions around who sees the panel, who has authority, and how changes are communicated to visitors.

Decision

Use persistent controls to make playback state and permissions legible at launch, then revisit raycast or remote controls once creator roles, ownership, and visitor feedback loops are clearer.
