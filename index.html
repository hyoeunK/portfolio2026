Meta Horizon Worlds Preteen Account & Parental Supervision UX Design

Designed a safer social VR experience for preteens, paired with parental supervision tools that helped guardians stay informed while supporting an age-appropriate experience.

Context

Meta Quest needed age-appropriate tools, content, and protections for younger users entering immersive social spaces, including play, exploration, creation, events, and friend-based social experiences.

Problem

For preteens, immersive social features carried heightened risk: unfamiliar people, voice chat, virtual proximity, embodied interaction, and potential exposure to inappropriate content.

Challenge

The core question was how to design safety UX that balanced preteen agency, parent trust, and platform enjoyment without turning supervision into simple monitoring or blunt restriction.

Approach

I reframed safety from access restriction into a trust system: clear defaults, parent-managed decisions, transparent signals, and guardrails that preserved the core fun of VR.

Outcome

The work helped define an age-appropriate experience model where preteens could participate with stronger protections, while parents had the visibility and controls needed to guide their child's use.

My Scope

I worked with Legal, Policy, Family Center, and a three-person design team. My scope focused on the core user journey and UXR alignment, including request/approve flows, content ratings, notifications, and GenAI parental supervision features.

Design Strategy

01

Design age-appropriate defaults instead of simply adding restrictions. Adults, teens, and preteens needed different onboarding, permissions, and protection models that built trust from the start.

02

Turn access blocks into role transitions. The approval flow connected the preteen's VR screen, parent notification, review details, and Family Center into one cross-surface request loop.

03

Redesign safety for spatial and voice-based interaction. VR required guardrails for garbled voice, personal boundaries, hidden status/location, and known-people-first discovery.

04

Combine content ratings, child requests, and parent judgment. For UGC, ratings provided decision context so parents could make informed choices instead of relying on one-size-fits-all restrictions.

05

Position Family Center as a conversation starter, not a control panel. The experience focused on decision-ready signals, usage context, approvals, and simple education instead of more surveillance.
